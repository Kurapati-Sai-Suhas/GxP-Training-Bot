# PART 2 — THE THREE EXPLANATIONS

## 60 SECONDS

> "GxP-regulated manufacturers have to retrain staff on standard operating procedures, and today
> that's mostly a slide deck and a generic quiz. If someone scores 60%, you know *they*
> struggled — you don't know *what* they struggled with.
>
> This system ingests a controlled SOP, splits it into sections, and uses an LLM to draft
> role-specific questions from one section at a time. Every draft goes to a subject-matter expert
> who approves it under an electronic signature bound to a hash of the exact content — the AI
> drafts, the human decides.
>
> Then the useful part. Because every question keeps a foreign key to the SOP section it came
> from, every answer is evidence about *that passage*. The system scores each section separately,
> flags the weak ones, and builds the next quiz from only those. In my demo the learner scores
> 33%, gets six questions back instead of nine, and improves from 50% to 100% on both weak
> sections — measured from their stored answers, not asserted.
>
> It's GxP-oriented, not GxP-compliant, and I can tell you exactly where that line is."

## 3 MINUTES — TECHNICAL

> "Django 5 with DRF on the backend, React 18 on the front, Postgres or SQLite, Redis and Celery,
> and NVIDIA NIM for inference — Llama 3.1 8B for generation, an embedding model for semantic
> chunking. Seven Django apps split by domain.
>
> **Ingestion.** PyMuPDF extracts text, then a three-tier chunking cascade: heading-aware regex
> first, because real SOPs are numbered; if that fails, Max-Min semantic chunking using
> embeddings; and fixed-length as a floor. Whichever tier ran is stored on the chunk, so a
> reviewer can see how a passage was cut.
>
> **Generation.** One chunk per prompt, with instructions to use only that text. Three retries
> with backoff, then a deterministic offline generator whose correct answer is lifted verbatim
> from the SOP. Failures are bucketed into nine categories. New drafts are checked against
> existing ones with lexical near-duplicate detection — answer similarity ≥0.8 *and* stem ≥0.4.
>
> **Review.** Drafts are invisible to learners; the queryset forces approved-only for non-
> reviewers, and learners get a different serializer that omits `is_correct` and the explanation
> entirely. Approval re-verifies the reviewer's password and stores a SHA-256 hash over canonical
> JSON of the content. After that the row is immutable — 403 on any write.
>
> **Assessment.** Submission is validated before anything is written: every question must belong
> to this attempt's SOP and role and be approved, no duplicates. Then an atomic compare-and-set
> claims the attempt, so a double submit gets a 409 rather than overwriting a training record.
> Grading is entirely server-side.
>
> **Adaptation.** Two mastery tables share an abstract base: `TopicMastery` per SOP,
> `ChunkMastery` per section. Each answered section gets an Elo ability update and an FSRS memory
> update. Selection then ranks sections by exponentially recency-weighted accuracy.
>
> 221 backend tests, migrations clean, deploy check clean, zero lint warnings."

## 5 MINUTES — ADAPTIVE LEARNING

> "The claim is that this adapts, so let me define what changes and on what evidence.
>
> **The unit.** Not the document — the section. `SOPChunk`. Every generated question carries a
> foreign key to the chunk it came from, so when a learner answers, that's evidence about a
> specific passage of a specific procedure. That FK is the whole architecture in one field.
>
> **The signal.** One boolean: `AttemptAnswer.is_correct`. Everything else is derived.
>
> **The metric.** For each section I take that learner's answer history newest-first and compute
> an exponentially recency-weighted accuracy: weight `0.5^(i/5)`, so an answer five back counts
> half as much. Five matches the 'recent' window shown in the UI, so 'recent' means one half-life
> everywhere.
>
> This replaced a flat lifetime average, and it fixed a real bug. A learner who answered 0/5 then
> 5/5 has plainly improved, but their lifetime average is 50% — so the screen showed 'Recent:
> 100%' next to a HIGH priority badge. It contradicted itself. Weighted, that learner is at 66.7%
> and moves to MEDIUM. Reversed — 5/5 then 0/5 — they drop to 33.3%, so decline is caught sooner
> too. Neither happens instantly: one good answer can't erase a history of failure, which is the
> right behaviour for a compliance record.
>
> **The decision.** Check order matters. Never assessed comes first and returns HIGH — absence of
> data must never read as competence. Then mastered, which retires a section after three
> consecutive passing assessments. Then an evidence gate: below three answers you cannot *exclude*
> a section, so strong performance on a tiny sample is capped at MEDIUM. That's deliberately
> asymmetric — weak performance on a small sample still reads HIGH, because over-training costs a
> few questions and under-training costs you an unqualified operator. Then the thresholds: below
> 60% is HIGH, below 80% is MEDIUM, otherwise LOW.
>
> **Two model-based components.** Elo runs per answer — learner K=32 so ability moves fast from
> few answers, question K=16 so shared difficulty moves slowly. FSRS-4.5 maintains stability and
> difficulty per section and schedules the next review at 90% predicted retention.
>
> **And they're deliberately separate.** Adaptive selection answers *what*; FSRS answers *when*.
> They cannot be merged, because FSRS retrievability `R(0,S) = 1.0` for every stability — right
> after an assessment, a section you just failed and one you just passed both read 1.0. So the
> learning path reports both: 'needs review' and 'available now' are different counts.
>
> **Measuring whether it worked.** Per section I compare the oldest half of the answer history
> against the newest half — at least four answers. In the demo that's 50% to 100%, +50 points, on
> both weak sections, from stored answers.
>
> **Classification, honestly.** The priority engine is rule-based over a statistic. Elo and FSRS
> are model-based and estimate parameters online. It's a hybrid, it's genuinely adaptive, and it
> is not machine learning — nothing is trained offline. The LLM writes questions; it has no vote
> in any adaptive decision."

# PART 4 — THE PREPARED ANSWERS

## 9. "Is your adaptive learning rule-based or ML?"

> "Both, and I'll be precise about which part is which.
>
> The **priority decision** — what to retrain — is **rule-based**: fixed thresholds at 60% and
> 80%, a minimum-evidence gate of three, and a mastery streak of three. Those numbers are chosen,
> not fitted.
>
> But they're applied to a **statistic estimated from data**: an exponentially recency-weighted
> accuracy per section, half-life five answers.
>
> And two components are genuinely **model-based with online parameter estimation**. Elo maintains
> a learner ability rating and a question difficulty rating, both updated per answer. FSRS-4.5
> maintains a memory model — stability and difficulty per section — and schedules from it.
>
> So: a **hybrid**. Rule-based decision layer, statistical signal, two model-based components. It
> is adaptive in the technical sense. It is **not machine learning** — nothing is trained offline,
> there is no dataset, no loss function, no gradient. I'd rather say that than overclaim."

## 10. "Why didn't you use a neural network?"

> "Data scale, and I can be specific about it.
>
> Deep knowledge tracing needs thousands of interactions per learner to beat a simple baseline.
> A GxP training deployment produces maybe a few dozen answers per person per SOP per year. At
> that scale a neural model would be fitting noise, and I'd have no way to tell.
>
> There's a second reason that matters more here. This is a regulated-training context. When the
> system says 'retrain Rohit on CAPA', a quality auditor can ask why, and the answer has to be a
> sentence a human can check: '0 out of 3 correct, below the 60% weakness threshold.' The system
> stores that sentence with every recommendation. A neural net would give me a number I couldn't
> defend.
>
> If I had the data I'd go to Bayesian Knowledge Tracing or IRT first — they're interpretable and
> far more sample-efficient. Deep learning would be last, not first."

## 11. "Why is this still AI/ML-related if the main adaptive decision is rule-based?"

> "Three honest reasons.
>
> First, the **generation** side is genuinely AI: an LLM drafting role-specific assessment items
> from controlled source text, plus an embedding model doing semantic chunking when heading
> detection fails. That's the AI-heavy half and I don't want to undersell it.
>
> Second, two of the adaptive components **are** parameter-estimating models. Elo is a latent-trait
> model fitted online — the same family as IRT, just with a cheap update rule. FSRS is a memory
> model whose 17 weights were fitted on hundreds of millions of reviews.
>
> Third — and this is the part I'd defend hardest — the interesting engineering problem here isn't
> 'which model'. It's **how you put a probabilistic system inside a regulated workflow safely**:
> where the human gate goes, what the LLM is allowed to decide, what has to stay deterministic and
> auditable. That's an AI systems problem, and it's most of the work."

## 12. "How is this different for two learners?"

> "Two learners on the same SOP get **verifiably different question sets** — there's a test named
> for exactly that, not just an isolation check.
>
> Everything is keyed on (learner, section). Learner A weak on CAPA and strong on GMP gets the
> CAPA questions; learner B with the opposite profile gets GMP's. Their Elo ability ratings
> diverge from the first attempt — in the demo, one attempt separates a strong section at ~1545
> from weak ones near 1450. Their FSRS schedules diverge too: failed sections come due sooner than
> passed ones, so the *timing* differs as well as the content.
>
> And it's not just different content — the amount differs. A learner who has demonstrated a
> section is never dragged back through it. Six questions instead of nine, in the demo."

## 13. "How do you measure whether adaptation actually helped?"

> "Per section, I split that learner's stored answer history in half — oldest half against newest
> half — and report the difference in percentage points. It needs at least four answers, because
> below that there aren't two halves worth comparing, and I'd rather show nothing than a number I
> can't support.
>
> In the demo both weak sections go from 50% to 100% — **+50 points** each — while the section
> that was already strong is left alone. That's computed from `AttemptAnswer` rows, not asserted.
>
> I'll be straight about what this is and isn't. It's a **within-learner pre/post measurement**,
> not a controlled experiment. I have no control group and no counterfactual, so I can show the
> learner improved on exactly the material the system selected — I can't prove they wouldn't have
> improved from re-reading the whole SOP. Establishing that needs an A/B trial against
> non-adaptive retraining, and that's a study, not a feature."

## 14. "How do you prevent hallucinated questions?"

> "I don't prevent them. I mitigate at three layers and prevent at one — and the one is the human.
>
> **Layer one, constrained generation:** one chunk per prompt, containing only that section's
> text, with instructions not to use outside knowledge.
>
> **Layer two, provenance:** every question stores a foreign key to its source chunk, and the
> reviewer sees that exact passage next to the question. Note what that is — it's provenance, not
> entailment. Nothing mechanically verifies the answer follows from the passage.
>
> **Layer three, the deterministic fallback:** if the provider fails after three retries, an
> offline generator takes the correct answer verbatim from the SOP text. Lower quality, but it
> cannot hallucinate.
>
> **The prevention layer is the SME.** No question reaches a learner without a qualified human
> approving it under electronic signature. Studies find up to 45% of LLM-generated MCQs contain
> implausible content — that's precisely why the gate is mandatory rather than advisory.
>
> The honest gap is entailment verification, and it's on my roadmap."

## 15. "Why is SME review necessary?"

> "Two reasons — one regulatory, one technical.
>
> **Regulatory:** in a GxP context, training content is controlled content. A qualified person has
> to be accountable for what an operator is assessed on. 'The model generated it' is not an
> answer an auditor accepts. So approval is bound to a named human, their re-verified password,
> and a hash of the exact content.
>
> **Technical:** the model has no ground truth. It's producing plausible text conditioned on a
> passage; nothing in the pipeline can verify the stated correct answer is actually correct.
> Confidence scores don't fix that — LLM self-reported confidence is known to be miscalibrated,
> which is why I use it only as a weak signal for weighting the pass calculation, never as an
> approval criterion.
>
> So the SME is not bureaucracy layered on a working system. It's the **only correctness control
> in the pipeline**, and the architecture is built around that fact: the LLM drafts, the human
> decides, and after they decide the content is frozen."

## 16. "Why use FSRS?"

> "The original implementation used Leitner boxes — a fixed 1/2/4/7/14/30-day ladder. That
> schedules every item identically regardless of how hard the material is or how fast that
> specific person forgets it.
>
> FSRS-4.5 replaces the ladder with an actual memory model: three variables — difficulty,
> stability, retrievability — with `R = (1 + F·t/S)^C`. It schedules the next review for the point
> where predicted recall drops to 90%. Its 17 weights are the published defaults, fitted on
> hundreds of millions of real reviews, which is far more data than this deployment will ever
> produce.
>
> I use only two of the four grades — AGAIN and GOOD — because my signal is binary correct or
> incorrect. I'd rather use the model honestly on the signal I have than invent a four-point scale
> I can't justify.
>
> The Leitner box index is still computed and shown, because it's a legible audit signal. It just
> no longer drives the schedule."

## 17. "Why use Elo?"

> "Because difficulty shouldn't be a label somebody typed once.
>
> Every question starts at 1500, seeded by its difficulty label. Every answer is a match: the
> learner's ability against the question's difficulty. Get a hard question right and your rating
> rises more than for an easy one; the question's own rating falls slightly.
>
> Two K-factors on purpose. Learner K=32, because a learner's ability estimate has to adapt from
> very few answers — that's what lets a transferring operator with real prior knowledge be
> recognised in a handful of questions instead of being treated like a first-day hire.
> Question K=16, because a question's rating is shared across every learner who ever sees it, so
> one person's bad day shouldn't move it much.
>
> It's used for two things: weighting the pass signal, so a hard question answered correctly
> counts for more toward mastery; and suggesting difficulty for the next assignment.
>
> One subtlety I'd flag — at section level I use an **ability-only** update. The whole-attempt pass
> already moved each question's difficulty once; doing it again per section would double-count a
> single answer.
>
> And I'll volunteer a real inconsistency: Elo weights the *pass signal* but not the *priority*.
> An easy question missed and a hard question missed currently count the same toward calling a
> section weak. That's a two-hour fix and it's next on my list — I didn't want to change the
> selection metric twice in one sprint and have two behaviour shifts to validate at once."

## 18. "Why separate adaptive selection from spaced repetition?"

> "Because they answer different questions, and there's a mathematical reason they can't be
> merged.
>
> Adaptive selection answers **what** — which sections is this learner weak on. Spaced repetition
> answers **when** — when is the right moment to review.
>
> The obvious move is to use FSRS retrievability for both: it's a 'how well is this remembered'
> number. It doesn't work. `R(elapsed, stability)` evaluates to **1.0 for every stability value
> when elapsed time is zero**. So immediately after an assessment — exactly when you want to
> decide what to retrain — a section you just failed and a section you just passed both score 1.0.
> FSRS cannot distinguish them at that moment.
>
> So selection uses observed accuracy, which does distinguish them, and FSRS keeps the scheduling.
> Each does what it's actually good at.
>
> The UI has to show both, and that's the bit I'd point at. A section can be HIGH priority but not
> yet due — the learning path says 'needs review' *and* 'scheduled for the 15th'. An earlier
> version showed only the first, so it recommended CAPA and then the quiz screen offered nothing.
> Found in audit, fixed, and there's a regression test for it."

## 19. "What is the biggest limitation of your system?"

> "The adaptive engine's exact offered question set is not persisted and enforced server-side.
>
> Here's what that means concretely. The server validates that every submitted question belongs to
> this attempt's SOP, matches the role, and is approved — I can show you the 400s. What it does
> **not** do is record which questions were actually *offered*. So a modified client can submit an
> approved question the engine excluded, or omit questions it doesn't want to answer. And because
> the score is computed over submitted answers, omitting inflates it.
>
> It's bounded — same SOP, approved content only, own attempts, everything audited — and the real
> client always submits the full offered set, sending null for anything unanswered, which grades
> as wrong. But I'm not going to pretend that's enforcement. **The adaptive decision is computed
> and validated server-side; it is not yet enforced.**
>
> There's a second consequence that matters as much: nothing records what was offered, so a past
> attempt can't be reconstructed as the learner sat it. That weakens the training record.
>
> The fix is one join table — `QuizAttemptQuestion` — written at attempt creation and checked at
> submission. It's first on my roadmap. I didn't ship a migration on the highest-consequence code
> path two days before this review, and I'd make that call again.
>
> What I *did* ship this week was the related fix I could make safely: duplicate question ids in
> one submission are now rejected with a 400 and zero writes. Before that, submitting the same
> question five times wrote five answer rows — enough to satisfy the minimum-evidence gate with a
> single question and inflate that section's accuracy. That one could actively corrupt the
> adaptive engine, so it was worth doing; the join table wasn't, this week."

## 20. "If you had another 3 months, what would you implement?"

> "In this order, and the ordering is the argument.
>
> **1. Server-side `QuizAttemptQuestion`** — persist the offered set, validate against it. This is
> first because it converts the adaptive decision from advisory to **enforced**, and makes attempts
> reproducible. It's the single strongest objection to the project and it's one model plus a
> migration.
>
> **2. SOP versioning** — a `SOPVersion` entity between document and chunk. Second because it's the
> only remaining gap that can *silently destroy learner data*: today reprocessing a revised SOP
> would cascade away every learner's `ChunkMastery`. It's currently blocked with a 409, which is a
> guard, not a workflow — revising a procedure has no supported path, and in a regulated
> environment that's an operational limit rather than a theoretical one.
>
> **3. Question revision history** — immutable version rows, signature bound to a version. Third
> because it completes content lineage: right now reject-edit-re-approve overwrites the superseded
> wording, so you can't reconstruct what a learner saw.
>
> Those three are the compliance-integrity block. Everything after is capability.
>
> **4. Difficulty-aware priority** — weight each answer by its question's live Elo inside the
> accuracy calculation. Two hours, removes a real internal inconsistency. It's fourth only because
> it's cheap enough to slot anywhere.
>
> **5. Semantic duplicate detection** — embedding cosine similarity alongside the lexical check,
> which today misses duplicates sharing no vocabulary.
>
> **6. Entailment verification** — an NLI model or judge scoring whether the correct answer is
> actually supported by its source chunk. This is the most technically interesting item and it
> upgrades grounding from provenance to verification — but it informs the SME, it never replaces
> them.
>
> **7. BKT then IRT** — BKT first because it's interpretable and sample-efficient; IRT once there
> are enough responses per item to calibrate. This is where the system would start genuinely
> learning its own parameters rather than using chosen thresholds.
>
> **8. Agentic orchestration** — a supervised pipeline where generation, de-duplication, and
> quality-scoring are separate steps with retries and explicit hand-offs. Last deliberately: it's
> the most fashionable item and the least load-bearing, and I wouldn't put an agent anywhere near
> the compliance controls.
>
> The through-line: **integrity first, then measurement quality, then intelligence.** Nothing above
> moves the human gate."

# PART 5 — HOSTILE FOLLOW-UPS

## "What prevents a learner from manipulating the API?"

> "Layered, and I'll tell you where the layers stop.
>
> Authentication and role scoping — a learner's queryset is filtered to their own attempts, so
> someone else's returns 404, not 403, so it doesn't even confirm the attempt exists. Submission
> validation rejects questions from another SOP, wrong role, unapproved drafts, and duplicate ids
> — all 400, all before any write. Resubmission is blocked by an atomic compare-and-set, so a
> completed training record can't be rewritten; the loser gets a 409 and it's audited. Grading is
> entirely server-side; the answer key never reaches the client, and I can show you the raw
> payload. Rate limits on login and signature verification.
>
> Where it stops: the offered set isn't pinned. Within their own SOP, within approved content,
> a modified client can substitute or omit questions. Bounded and audited — but not prevented."

## "Can the learner change the adaptive question selection?"

> "Not the *decision* — that's computed server-side from stored answers and they have no input
> into it. But they can partly evade the *consequence*, because the offered set isn't pinned. They
> could answer an approved question the engine excluded.
>
> What they cannot do: reach another SOP, reach unapproved content, reach another learner's
> attempt, resubmit a completed attempt, see the answer key, or repeat one question to farm
> evidence — that last one I fixed this week specifically because it could corrupt the engine."

## "Is the adaptive engine actually AI?"

> "The adaptive engine is not a neural model, and I won't dress it up as one. It's a rule-based
> decision layer over a recency-weighted statistic, with two model-based components — Elo and
> FSRS — that estimate parameters online from responses. Elo is a latent-trait model, the same
> family as IRT.
>
> The AI in the system is the generation pipeline: an LLM drafting from controlled source text and
> an embedding model doing semantic chunking. I keep those two things separate on purpose,
> because the LLM has no vote in any adaptive decision."

## "Are you using machine learning?"

> "No — not in the sense that matters. Nothing is trained offline, there's no dataset, no loss
> function, no gradient step.
>
> What I do have is **online parameter estimation**: Elo ratings and FSRS memory state update from
> every answer. That's learning from data in the statistical sense, not model training. I'd rather
> draw that line clearly than claim ML and have you find out it's thresholds."

## "Where is the model trained?"

> "Nowhere — and that's a deliberate answer, not a gap I'm hiding.
>
> The LLM is a hosted NVIDIA NIM endpoint; I do no fine-tuning. FSRS's 17 weights are published
> defaults fitted by its authors on hundreds of millions of reviews — I use them as-is. Elo has no
> training phase by construction; ratings update per answer.
>
> The parameters I *did* choose — 60/80% thresholds, half-life 5, minimum evidence 3 — are
> documented as chosen defaults, not fitted values. With real deployment data, MIN_EVIDENCE is the
> first one I'd tune."

## "What happens if the LLM generates a wrong question?"

> "It gets rejected by the SME and never reaches a learner. That's the whole point of the gate
> being mandatory.
>
> If a wrong question somehow got approved, the consequence is bounded but real: it becomes one
> answer among others in that section's history, weighted at most 1.0 in the recency calculation,
> and the minimum-evidence gate means a single item can't exclude a section on its own. Elo would
> also drift it toward looking anomalously hard as learners miss it.
>
> What I *don't* have is automatic detection — no entailment check. The reviewer is the control,
> and rejecting a question is one click."

## "Can an SME modify an approved question?"

> "No. PATCH, PUT and DELETE on an approved question all return 403.
>
> That's because the electronic signature is bound to a SHA-256 hash of the exact content —
> question text, explanation, difficulty, and every option including which is correct. Allowing an
> edit would silently invalidate the signature, so the system refuses rather than tracks it. And
> if something modifies the row by another route — the ORM, the admin, direct SQL —
> `signature_is_intact()` recomputes the hash and reports the mismatch.
>
> The honest limitation: to change an approved question you reject and re-approve, and that
> overwrites the superseded wording. Proper version rows are item three on my roadmap."

## "What happens when an SOP changes?"

> "Today: it's blocked. Reprocessing a document that has approved questions returns 409.
>
> I'll be direct that this is a guard, not a workflow. Reprocessing would delete the chunks,
> cascade away every learner's `ChunkMastery`, and orphan approved questions from their source
> text. Blocking it prevents data loss; it doesn't give you a revision path.
>
> So **revising a procedure has no supported path**, which in a regulated environment is a real
> operational limit. The fix is a `SOPVersion` entity between document and chunk, so publishing v2
> leaves v1's chunks, questions and mastery intact, and a policy decides whether the revision
> triggers requalification. It's second on my roadmap and it deserves a sprint of its own — it's
> the highest-risk item in the backlog."

## "How do you prove the learner improved?"

> "I compare the oldest half of that section's stored answers against the newest half, in
> percentage points, needing at least four answers. In the demo: 50% to 100%, +50 points, on both
> weak sections, computed from `AttemptAnswer` rows.
>
> Now the honest boundary. That's a **within-learner pre/post measurement**, not proof that
> adaptation caused it. No control group, no counterfactual. I can show they improved on exactly
> the material the system selected. I cannot show they wouldn't have improved by re-reading the
> whole SOP. Proving that needs an A/B trial against non-adaptive retraining — that's a study, not
> a feature, and I'd rather name the limit than overclaim the result."

## "Why should I trust your adaptive recommendation?"

> "Don't trust it — check it. That's why every recommendation carries its evidence.
>
> The learning path shows, per section: the adaptive score the decision was made on, the lifetime
> accuracy, the recent accuracy, how many answers there are, and a sentence naming the threshold
> that fired — '0% accuracy, 0 of 3 correct, below the 60% weakness threshold.' Both figures are
> shown deliberately, so the screen can never contradict its own verdict.
>
> Underneath, every number traces to `AttemptAnswer` rows, each tied to a question tied by foreign
> key to a specific SOP section. There's no step where a model produces an unexplainable score.
>
> That's also why the decision layer is rule-based. In a regulated context, 'the network said so'
> isn't defensible to an auditor. A threshold and a count are."

## "Why isn't this just if-else?"

> "The classification *is* if-else — four branches, and I'd defend that as the right design for a
> compliance decision. But the interesting part isn't the branch, it's what's being branched on.
>
> The input is an exponentially recency-weighted accuracy over an ordered answer history, computed
> per source section, gated on evidence sufficiency, reconciled against a spaced-repetition
> schedule, with two online-estimated rating systems feeding the mastery signal.
>
> Take the recency weighting alone — that's not a condition, it's a metric design decision that
> fixed a real bug: a flat average can't represent *change*, so a learner who went 0/5 then 5/5 was
> still flagged weak while the screen showed 'Recent: 100%'.
>
> So: the last step is if-else, and deliberately so — it's auditable. Everything that makes it
> correct happens before it."

## "Why don't you use deep learning?"

> "Sample size and defensibility.
>
> Deep knowledge tracing needs thousands of interactions per learner to beat simple baselines. This
> deployment produces a few dozen answers per person per SOP. A neural model would fit noise, and
> in a compliance setting I'd have no way to detect that it had.
>
> And when the system tells a quality manager to retrain someone, that has to survive being
> questioned. 'Below the 60% threshold on 3 answers' survives. A softmax output doesn't.
>
> If the data existed I'd go BKT, then IRT, then neural — in that order, interpretability first.
> Declining deep learning at this data scale is a decision I can defend, not a gap."

## THE THREE THAT DECIDE THE VIVA

**"Why is this adaptive?"** → *"The content selected for the next assessment changes based on
measured per-section performance. The learner scores 33% on nine questions and gets six back —
only the two weak sections. Because every question keeps a foreign key to the SOP chunk it came
from, every answer is evidence about that passage, not about the document."*

**"Can I bypass your adaptive decision?"** → *"Partly, and I'll be precise. The server validates
every submitted question belongs to this SOP, is approved, and isn't a duplicate — I can show you
the 400s. What it doesn't do yet is pin the exact set that was offered. Bounded: same SOP,
approved content, own attempts, all audited. The fix is one join table. I didn't ship a migration
two days before review."*

**"Is this GxP compliant?"** → *"No, and I wouldn't claim it. It's GxP-**oriented**: several Part
11-**style** technical controls — password-verified approval bound to a content hash, attributed
audit trail, RBAC, immutable approved content. Missing: tamper-evident audit storage, separation
of duties, a qualification record, and any formal validation exercise."*
