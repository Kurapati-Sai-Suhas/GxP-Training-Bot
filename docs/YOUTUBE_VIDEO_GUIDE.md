# SCRIPT

## 0:00 — HOOK (say this over the title slide)

> "In pharmaceutical manufacturing, every operator has to be trained on standard operating
> procedures — and retrained, repeatedly. Today that's usually one slide deck and one quiz for
> everybody.
>
> The problem is that if someone scores 60%, you know *they* struggled. You don't know *what*
> they struggled with.
>
> This is GxP Training Bot. It reads a controlled SOP, uses an LLM to draft assessment questions
> from specific sections of it, sends every one of those to a human expert for approval under
> electronic signature — and then uses each learner's performance, section by section, to decide
> what they should be retrained on next.
>
> Let me show you it working."

## 0:40 — THE PROBLEM (slide 2)

> "Three things pull against each other here.
>
> Automation wants the AI to just get on with it. Regulation wants a named human accountable for
> any content an operator is assessed on. And personalisation wants every learner to get something
> different.
>
> Most systems pick one. The question I set out to answer was whether you can have all three —
> automated, human-validated, explainable, and adaptive."

## 1:40 — ARCHITECTURE (slide 6)

> "Top row is the content pipeline. The SOP comes in, gets extracted and split into chunks, the AI
> drafts questions from those chunks, and then everything stops at the SME gate. Nothing reaches
> a learner without a human approving it.
>
> Below that is the adaptive half — and notice it's two engines, not one. One decides *what* to
> train. The other decides *when*. I'll explain why those can't be the same thing later."

## 2:40 — DEMO PART 1 *(switch to the live app)*

**Show: SOP Library**
> "This is a controlled SOP with three sections — good manufacturing practice, CAPA and root cause
> analysis, and documentation practices. The system detected the numbered headings, so each chunk
> maps to a real section of the procedure rather than an arbitrary character cut."

**Show: Question Review — the badges**
> "Nine questions, three per section, generated by Llama 3.1 8B running on NVIDIA NIM."

**Show: click the source-text disclosure** ⭐
> "This is the part I care most about. That's the exact source passage the question was generated
> from, stored alongside it. So a reviewer can always answer 'where did this come from?'
>
> And I want to be precise: this is provenance plus a prompt constraint. **It is not verified
> entailment.** Nothing mechanically checks that the correct answer follows from that passage.
> That's what the human is for."

**Show / describe: approval**
> "Approving requires the reviewer to re-enter their own password, checked on the server. On
> success the system stores a SHA-256 hash of the exact content approved — including which option
> is correct. After that the question is immutable; any attempt to edit it returns a 403."

## 4:30 — DEMO PART 2 *(learner account)*

**Show: My Learning Path after the first quiz**
> "The learner has taken one quiz and scored 33%. But look at what the system knows instead of
> just a percentage.
>
> Two sections flagged HIGH priority, in red — and each one carries the evidence: the adaptive
> score the decision was made on, the lifetime accuracy, how many answers there are, and a
> sentence naming the threshold that fired.
>
> The third section is green. 100%. **Excluded.** The learner will not be dragged back through it.
>
> Now notice this badge — 'Scheduled for the 15th', and up here, 'zero available now'. These
> sections need work, but the spaced-repetition scheduler says not yet. Two algorithms, two
> different questions, and the interface reports both rather than promising a quiz it can't
> deliver."

**Switch to terminal, run the full demo**
```bash
uv run python manage.py demo_adaptive
```
> "I'm running the whole loop end to end. It fast-forwards the schedule — only the schedule — and
> every score, every mastery update, every selection comes from the real engine."

**Point at steps 9 and 11 in the output** ⭐
> "**Six questions selected, not nine.** The strong section was excluded.
>
> And after retraining: both weak sections go from **50% to 100% — fifty percentage points.**
> That's computed from stored answers, comparing the oldest half of each section's history against
> the newest half. It isn't asserted, it's measured."

## 6:20 — HOW THE ADAPTIVE ENGINE WORKS (slides 10, 11, 12)

> "So how does it decide? For each section I take that learner's answers newest-first and compute
> a recency-weighted accuracy — an answer five back counts half as much as the most recent one.
>
> Why weight it? Because a flat average can't represent *change*. Someone who went 0 out of 5 and
> then 5 out of 5 has obviously improved, but their lifetime average is 50% — so the screen was
> showing 'Recent: 100%' next to a HIGH priority badge, contradicting itself. Weighted, they're at
> 66.7% and move down a band.
>
> Below 60% is HIGH, below 80% is MEDIUM, above that is LOW, and three consecutive passes retires
> the section entirely. There's also an evidence gate — under three answers you can't *exclude* a
> section, though you can still flag it as weak. That asymmetry is deliberate: over-training costs
> a few extra questions, under-training costs you an unqualified operator."

**Slide 11 — say this carefully**
> "I should be precise about what kind of system this is, because it'd be easy to overclaim.
>
> It's a **hybrid**. The priority engine is rule-based — thresholds and gates, chosen not fitted.
> But it operates on a statistic estimated from data. And two components genuinely learn online:
> **Elo**, which estimates learner ability and question difficulty from every answer, and **FSRS**,
> a memory model that drives scheduling.
>
> So — the LLM does **not** determine adaptive priority. The adaptive engine is **not** a trained
> neural network. It's adaptive because learner performance changes future content selection.
>
> And people ask why I didn't use deep learning. Two reasons. Deep knowledge tracing needs
> thousands of interactions per learner; this produces a few dozen. And in a regulated setting,
> when the system says 'retrain this person on CAPA', that has to survive being questioned by an
> auditor. 'Below the 60% threshold on three answers' survives that. A neural network's output
> doesn't."

**Slide 12 — the R(0,S) point**
> "One more thing, and it's my favourite detail. The obvious move is to use the spaced-repetition
> model's retrievability score for both jobs. It doesn't work — retrievability at zero elapsed
> time is 1.0 for *every* stability value. Right after an assessment, a section you just failed
> and one you just passed both score 1.0. It literally cannot distinguish them. So accuracy
> selects, and FSRS schedules."

## 7:50 — RESULTS (slide 13)

> "To be clear about what this is: a **verified demonstration result** from a controlled scenario.
> One learner, one SOP. It is not a user study and I'm not claiming statistical significance —
> that would need an A/B trial against non-adaptive retraining.
>
> What I can say is the loop works end to end: 221 backend tests passing, zero failures,
> migrations and deployment checks clean, and the whole workflow verified against the live model
> provider."

## 9:00 — LIMITATIONS (slide 15)

> "The biggest limitation, and I'd rather say it than have it found: the exact set of questions
> the engine *offered* isn't persisted server-side yet. Validation confirms every submitted
> question belongs to the right SOP, role and approved pool — but a modified client could submit a
> different eligible question, or omit some. So the adaptive decision is computed and validated
> server-side; it isn't yet *enforced*. The fix is one join table, and it's first on my roadmap.
>
> After that: SOP versioning, because right now revising a procedure has no supported path. Then
> content lineage, then entailment verification for the generated questions.
>
> Integrity first, then measurement quality, then intelligence."

## 9:50 — CLOSE (slide 16)

> "So, four sentences:
>
> **AI generates the content. SMEs control the content. Learner data drives adaptation. FSRS
> controls review timing.**
>
> The code, the architecture docs and the full audit are in the repository linked below. Thanks
> for watching."

# RECORDING SETUP

**Software — all free**
- **OBS Studio** — screen + mic, records to MP4. Set canvas to 1920×1080, 30fps.
- Alternatively **Windows Game Bar** (`Win + G`) for a quick single-window capture, or **Zoom**
  (record a meeting with yourself, share screen) if OBS feels heavy.

**Settings**
- 1080p, 30fps is plenty. Don't record at 4K — huge files, no benefit.
- Set your display to 1920×1080 before recording so text isn't tiny.
- Increase browser zoom to ~110–125% and terminal font to ~16pt. **Text that's readable on your
  monitor is often unreadable on a phone.**

**Audio matters more than video**
- Use any headset mic rather than the laptop's built-in one.
- Record in a small room with soft furnishings; avoid echo.
- Speak about 15% slower than feels natural.

**Recording approach — do it in takes**
Record each section separately and stitch them. A 10-minute single take means restarting from zero
every time you fumble. Sections are ~60–110 seconds each — very manageable.

**Editing** — free options: **DaVinci Resolve** (powerful), **Clipchamp** (built into Windows,
simple), or **CapCut**. You only need cuts, not effects.
